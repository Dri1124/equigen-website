High cholesterol isn’t always about what you eat.

Familial hypercholesterolemia (FH) is an inherited condition that can make it harder for the body to remove LDL cholesterol from the blood. Recognizing it early can help individuals and families take action.

FH: When the LDL-clearing system doesn't work as expected

FH is an inherited condition associated with high LDL cholesterol from birth. The key problem is often impaired removal of LDL particles from the bloodstream.

Imagine the liver as a busy recycling center

LDL particles circulate in blood carrying cholesterol. ApoB-100 acts as a recognizable surface ligand. LDL receptors on liver cells bind LDL, bring it into the cell, and normally return to the surface to work again.

LDLR-, APOB- and PCSK9-related FH are usually autosomal dominant: a person with one heterozygous pathogenic variant has a 50% chance of passing that variant to each child. LDLRAP1-related FH is typically autosomal recessive.

FH is not simply “cholesterol from food.” Lifestyle still matters for cardiovascular health, but FH begins with inherited biology that can make LDL clearance inefficient. Early recognition and treatment can substantially change risk.

How the liver normally clears LDL

Normally, the liver removes LDL particles from the bloodstream using LDL receptors. Think of them as recycling stations: LDL circulates in blood → a receptor recognizes the particle → the liver takes it in → LDL is removed from circulation. In many people with FH, this process is less efficient.

Four genes associated with FH

LDLR — receptor problem

The most commonly identified FH gene. Pathogenic variants may result in too few functional LDL receptors or receptors that work poorly.

APOB — binding problem

Apolipoprotein B-100 is part of the LDL particle and helps it bind its receptor. Certain variants interfere with that binding—like a key that no longer fits.

PCSK9 — receptor breakdown

Certain gain-of-function variants increase LDL receptor degradation, leaving fewer receptors available to clear LDL.

LDLRAP1 — internalization problem

This gene helps receptor-bound LDL enter liver cells. LDLRAP1-related FH is typically autosomal recessive and involves pathogenic variants in both gene copies.

Heterozygous and severe forms of FH

Most people with FH have heterozygous FH (HeFH), often involving one altered copy of an FH-related gene. Rarely, variants affecting both copies or particular combinations of variants can produce homozygous or severe FH, sometimes with very high LDL and cardiovascular disease beginning in childhood.

Can FH still be present after a negative genetic test?

Yes. Clinicians can diagnose FH using LDL levels, family history, premature cardiovascular disease, physical findings, and established criteria. Current tests do not identify a causative variant in every person with clinically diagnosed FH. A negative result should be interpreted alongside the clinical evidence.

WHY THIS MATTERS FOR BLACK WOMEN

Representation changes what we know—and who benefits.

Heart health & FH

Inherited high LDL can be missed when high cholesterol is framed only as a lifestyle issue. Knowing family history, LDL levels and whether premature heart disease runs in a family can help start a more informed conversation about FH.

EquiGen’s message: ancestry and identity are part of a person’s health context, but race itself is not a gene. Equitable genetics means better representation, access, interpretation, education and follow-through.

03 · TURN KNOWLEDGE INTO ACTION

What can I do today?

These are starting points for an informed conversation, not a personal diagnosis or treatment plan.

STEP 01

Know your numbers

Find your most recent lipid panel—or ask your healthcare professional to order one if you have never been tested. Identify LDL cholesterol, HDL cholesterol, triglycerides, and total cholesterol. If untreated LDL has repeatedly been around or above 190 mg/dL in an adult, ask whether FH should be considered.

STEP 02

Build your cardiovascular family history

Ask relatives about high cholesterol, heart attacks, coronary artery disease, stents or bypass surgery, stroke, and sudden or unexplained cardiovascular deaths. Record the age at each event; unusually early disease in multiple relatives can be a clue.

STEP 03

Consider specialist evaluation

Ask whether a lipidologist, preventive cardiologist, cardiologist, or other clinician experienced in lipid disorders would be helpful.

STEP 04

Discuss genetic counseling and testing

Testing may clarify the diagnosis and identify a familial pathogenic variant that can guide targeted testing of relatives. A negative result does not automatically rule out clinically diagnosed FH.

STEP 05

Ask about Lp(a)

Lipoprotein(a), or Lp(a), is a genetically influenced cardiovascular risk factor distinct from standard LDL cholesterol and generally requires a separate test.

STEP 06

Keep healthy habits—and discuss treatment

Nutrition, physical activity, avoiding tobacco, and managing blood pressure and blood glucose remain important. In FH, lifestyle changes alone are often not enough to lower LDL to an appropriate level; medication may be needed.
