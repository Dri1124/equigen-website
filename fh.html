START HERE · YOUR HEALTH JOURNEY

High cholesterol isn’t always about what you eat.

Familial hypercholesterolemia (FH) is an inherited condition that can make it harder for the body to remove LDL cholesterol from the blood. Recognizing it early can help individuals and families take action.

What you'll learn: how this condition affects the body, practical next steps, and questions to bring to a healthcare visit.
UNDERSTANDING YOUR RESULTS

Understanding Your Cholesterol Numbers

A lipid panel is a blood test that measures different fats and cholesterol-related values. These numbers can help you and your doctor understand cardiovascular risk. In familial hypercholesterolemia (FH), LDL cholesterol is usually the central concern.

LDL cholesterol

Low-density lipoprotein · often called “bad” cholesterol

LDL particles carry cholesterol through the bloodstream to cells that need it. When too much LDL cholesterol remains in the blood, LDL particles can enter artery walls and contribute to plaque buildup.

Connection to FH: Genetic changes can reduce the liver’s ability to clear LDL particles, leading to high LDL cholesterol from an early age.

HDL cholesterol

High-density lipoprotein · often called “good” cholesterol

HDL particles help move cholesterol from tissues toward the liver for processing and removal. HDL cholesterol is one part of the overall cardiovascular risk picture.

Important: A higher HDL cholesterol result does not cancel out the risk associated with very high LDL cholesterol.

Triglycerides

A different type of fat · a source and store of energy

The body stores unused energy as triglycerides and releases it when needed. High triglycerides can accompany other metabolic and cardiovascular risk factors.

Connection to FH: Triglycerides are often within the normal range in people with FH, even when LDL cholesterol is high.

Total cholesterol

A combined cholesterol measurement

Total cholesterol includes cholesterol carried in LDL, HDL and other lipoproteins. It can be a useful starting point, but it does not show which particles account for the result.

Why it matters: Looking at LDL, HDL and triglycerides separately gives a more informative picture than total cholesterol alone.

LDL cholesterol (LDL-C) versus LDL particles:

LDL is a particle that transports cholesterol; LDL-C is the amount of cholesterol carried by LDL particles. The LDL receptor helps cells, especially liver cells, remove LDL particles from circulation. The pathway below shows how this works.

Consider asking your doctor: “What do my LDL, HDL and triglyceride results mean together, and should I be evaluated for FH?”

FH: When the LDL-clearing system doesn't work as expected

FH is an inherited condition associated with high LDL cholesterol from birth. The key problem is often impaired removal of LDL particles from the bloodstream.

Imagine the liver as a busy recycling center

LDL particles circulate in blood carrying cholesterol. ApoB-100 acts as a recognizable surface ligand. LDL receptors on liver cells bind LDL, bring it into the cell, and normally return to the surface to work again.

Atherosclerosis (plaque buildup): Cholesterol and other substances can build up in artery walls over time. This may narrow the arteries and raise the risk of heart attacks, strokes, and other circulation problems. In FH, high LDL cholesterol from a young age can increase this risk.

LDLR-, APOB- and PCSK9-related FH are usually autosomal dominant: a person with one heterozygous pathogenic variant has a 50% chance of passing that variant to each child. LDLRAP1-related FH is typically autosomal recessive.

FH is not simply “cholesterol from food.” Lifestyle still matters for cardiovascular health, but FH begins with inherited biology that can make LDL clearance inefficient. Early recognition and treatment can substantially change risk.

Four genes associated with FH

LDLR — receptor problem

The most commonly identified FH gene. Pathogenic variants may result in too few functional LDL receptors or receptors that work poorly.

APOB — binding problem

Apolipoprotein B-100 is part of the LDL particle and helps it bind its receptor. Certain variants interfere with that binding—like a key that no longer fits.

PCSK9 — receptor breakdown

Certain gain-of-function variants increase LDL receptor degradation, leaving fewer receptors available to clear LDL.

LDLRAP1 — internalization problem

This gene helps receptor-bound LDL enter liver cells. LDLRAP1-related FH is typically autosomal recessive and involves pathogenic variants in both gene copies.

Heterozygous and severe forms of FH

  • Heterozygous FH (HeFH): The most common form of FH, often involving one altered copy of an FH-related gene.
  • Homozygous or severe FH: Rarely, variants affecting both copies of a gene or particular combinations of variants can lead to much higher LDL cholesterol levels and cardiovascular disease beginning in childhood.

Can FH still be present after a negative genetic test?

Yes.

  • Diagnosis: Doctors can diagnose FH using LDL cholesterol levels, family history, early cardiovascular disease, physical findings, and established diagnostic criteria.
  • Test limitations: Current genetic tests do not identify a causative variant in everyone who has clinically diagnosed FH.
  • What a negative result means: A negative genetic test does not automatically rule out FH. Results should be considered alongside your medical and family history.
WHY THIS MATTERS FOR BLACK WOMEN

Representation changes what we know—and who benefits.

Heart health & FH

Inherited high LDL can be missed when high cholesterol is framed only as a lifestyle issue. Knowing family history, LDL levels and whether premature heart disease runs in a family can help start a more informed conversation about FH.

EquiGen’s message: ancestry and identity are part of a person’s health context, but race itself is not a gene. Equitable genetics means better representation, access, interpretation, education and follow-through.

03 · TURN KNOWLEDGE INTO ACTION

What can I do today?

These are starting points for an informed conversation, not a personal diagnosis or treatment plan.

STEP 01

Know your numbers

Find your most recent lipid panel—or ask your doctor to order one if you have never been tested. Identify LDL cholesterol, HDL cholesterol, triglycerides, and total cholesterol. If untreated LDL has repeatedly been around or above 190 mg/dL in an adult, ask whether FH should be considered.

STEP 02

Build your cardiovascular family history

Ask relatives about high cholesterol, heart attacks, coronary artery disease, stents or bypass surgery, stroke, and sudden or unexplained cardiovascular deaths. Record the age at each event; unusually early disease in multiple relatives can be a clue.

STEP 03

Consider specialist evaluation

Ask whether a lipidologist, preventive cardiologist, cardiologist, or other doctor experienced in lipid disorders would be helpful.

STEP 04

Discuss genetic counseling and testing

Testing may clarify the diagnosis and identify a familial pathogenic variant that can guide targeted testing of relatives. A negative result does not automatically rule out clinically diagnosed FH.

STEP 05

Ask about Lp(a)

Lipoprotein(a), or Lp(a), is a genetically influenced cardiovascular risk factor distinct from standard LDL cholesterol and generally requires a separate test.

STEP 06

Keep healthy habits—and discuss treatment

Nutrition, physical activity, avoiding tobacco, and managing blood pressure and blood glucose remain important. In FH, lifestyle changes alone are often not enough to lower LDL to an appropriate level; medication may be needed.
