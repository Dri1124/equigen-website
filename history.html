Why We’re Talking About Trust

For generations, Black communities have experienced both extraordinary advances in medicine and profound injustices within healthcare and medical research. Understanding this history matters.

Medical mistrust did not develop without context. It has been shaped by documented experiences of exploitation, exclusion, discrimination, lack of informed consent, and unequal treatment.

EquiGen’s approach: Building healthier relationships with healthcare should not require forgetting the past. We can understand what happened, learn what protections exist today, recognize where inequities remain, and ask informed questions about our care.

Beyond the familiar stories

Explore Four Chapters of Medical History

Inspired in part by Harriet A. Washington’s Medical Apartheid, this expanded timeline introduces less-discussed episodes alongside major ethical reforms. Choose a chapter, open a story, and consider the question it raises for healthcare today.

A note on historical accuracy: These are independently sourced educational summaries, not excerpts from the book. A model law such as the Uniform Anatomical Gift Act is not itself an abuse, and laws governing donations vary by state. Historical wrongs should not be confused with every form of modern clinical care or research.

1800s

Anatomical exploitation and grave robbing

In the nineteenth century, some medical schools obtained bodies through grave robbing and the dissection of people who had little power to object. Poor and Black burial grounds were especially vulnerable. Families could be denied the ability to bury and mourn loved ones with dignity. This history raises questions about whose bodies were treated as available for medical teaching and how institutions should acknowledge those harmed.

Ask: How should institutions document the origins of human remains and respond to descendants?

1845–1849

Anarcha, Lucy, Betsey and gynecologic surgery

From 1845 to 1849, J. Marion Sims repeatedly operated on enslaved Black women in Alabama while developing surgery for childbirth-related fistulas. Anarcha, Lucy and Betsey are among the few whose names were recorded; Anarcha underwent about 30 operations. The women faced serious, painful conditions, yet enslavement denied them independent legal control over their bodies. Sims's own accounts cannot establish what each woman freely wanted or could safely refuse. Their labor, suffering and contributions deserve recognition alongside the medical technique.

Why this matters today + source

Ask: Can consent be freely given when another person has legal power over your body?

1929–1974

North Carolina sterilization program

North Carolina's 1929 sterilization law was ruled unconstitutional, but a new law established the Eugenics Board in 1933. From 1929 to 1974, about 7,600 people were sterilized under state authority, through procedures described by the state as voluntary, forced or coerced. The program targeted people labeled unfit, and its later decades increasingly affected Black residents, including women and girls. The state's later compensation program acknowledged the injustice; its claims process is now closed.

Why this matters today + source

Ask: Who decides whether a medical intervention is truly voluntary?

1932–1972

The Tuskegee syphilis study

U.S. Public Health Service researchers misled Black male participants and failed to offer adequate treatment, including after penicillin became standard care. Participants were not intentionally infected by researchers.

Why this matters today + source

Ask: How can participants confirm what treatment or alternatives are available?

1951

Henrietta Lacks and HeLa

In 1951, cervical cancer cells were taken from Henrietta Lacks at Johns Hopkins Hospital without her knowledge or consent for research. The cells became HeLa, a line that transformed biomedical science. Decades later, her family raised concerns about recognition, privacy and the use of her genetic information. In 2013, NIH and members of the Lacks family agreed on a process for controlled access to certain HeLa genome data. The agreement does not mean every use of HeLa cells requires family approval.

Why this matters today + source

Ask: What permission is requested for storage, sharing, or future research use of a sample?

1951–1974

Holmesburg Prison experiments

From 1951 into the 1970s, researchers used incarcerated people at Philadelphia's Holmesburg Prison in skin, drug and chemical experiments. Many participants were Black men. Payments, confinement and limited information about risks raised serious questions about voluntary informed consent. Former prisoners reported injuries and lasting harm. This history illustrates why protections for incarcerated research participants must address power and financial pressure, not simply signed forms.

Why this matters today + source

Ask: Can a person freely decline research when confined or financially pressured?

1947

The Nuremberg Code

Following the Doctors’ Trial, the Nuremberg Code articulated the central importance of voluntary consent in human experimentation. It did not automatically eliminate unethical research.

Why this matters today + source

Ask: What makes research consent voluntary and informed?

1968

Uniform Anatomical Gift Act (UAGA)

The 1968 Uniform Anatomical Gift Act offered states a model legal framework for donating organs or bodies after death for transplantation, therapy, research and education. It was a reform in donation authorization, not an example of medical abuse. The model itself was not automatically binding law: states adopted versions of it, and rules about who can authorize a gift and how objections are honored vary.

Why this matters today + source

Ask: Who can authorize an anatomical gift, and what happens if the person objected?

1974

National Research Act

Passed in 1974 after mounting concern about unethical human experimentation, the National Research Act established the National Commission for the Protection of Human Subjects of Biomedical and Behavioral Research and helped develop the framework for modern oversight. Institutional review boards evaluate many research studies, but oversight does not remove every risk or guarantee equitable treatment.

Why this matters today + source

Ask: Who reviews the risks and benefits of a proposed study?

1979

Belmont Report

The 1979 Belmont Report articulated three principles for research involving people: respect for persons (including informed choice and added protections for those with limited autonomy), beneficence (weighing potential benefits and harms), and justice (fair selection and treatment of participants). These principles remain influential, though implementation and enforcement still matter.

Why this matters today + source

Ask: Does this study recruit fairly, minimize harm, and respect participants’ choices?

1987 / 2006

Revisions to the UAGA

Revised model acts updated procedures for anatomical gifts and authorization. A model law is not automatically binding everywhere; applicable state law and the kind of donation matter.

Why this matters today + source

Ask: Does a body donation for education differ from a clinical tissue sample or an organ donor registration?

1990s–today

Genetic data, privacy and representation

Genetic research can improve prevention, diagnosis and treatment, but DNA data may reveal information about relatives as well as participants. Questions include how long samples and data are stored, who can access them, whether they may be reused, and whether people with African ancestry are adequately represented in research. Privacy protections differ by setting, and removing names does not eliminate every re-identification risk.

Why this matters today + source

Ask: What happens to my sample and data if I withdraw from a study?

What Does This Have to Do With Genetics?

Genetic information can be powerful. It can also feel deeply personal. Asking questions about what happens to your DNA is reasonable.

My DNA

Why is this test being recommended? What genes or conditions are being evaluated? Could the result reveal information relevant to my relatives?

My Sample

Will my blood, saliva or tissue be stored? For how long? Can I decline research use? What happens to the sample when testing is complete?

My Data

Who can access my genetic information? Will it be de-identified? Can it be shared with researchers or placed in a database?

Representation

People of African ancestry have historically been underrepresented in many genomic datasets. Representation matters because genetic variation differs across populations and influences what science is able to learn and interpret.

What about GINA? The federal Genetic Information Nondiscrimination Act generally prohibits genetic-information discrimination in health insurance and employment, but it does not provide the same protections for life, disability, or long-term-care insurance. Other laws and state protections may also apply.

Your Rights. Your Questions. Your Choice.

Trust does not have to mean accepting everything without question. Informed healthcare includes understanding your options and participating in decisions about your body.

A note on scientific sources, public trust & transparency

Using Federal Health Resources With Informed Trust

Agencies such as the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), National Institutes of Health (NIH), and other federal public health institutions have made foundational contributions to medical research, disease prevention, laboratory science, and public health. Their scientific work remains an important resource for doctors, researchers, and communities.

At the same time, public-facing websites and historical accounts may not tell the whole story. Information can be incomplete, revised, removed, or shaped by changing institutional priorities and government administrations. These limitations matter, particularly when researching the experiences of Black women and communities whose contributions, mistreatment, or concerns have historically been overlooked.

EquiGen may reference federal resources because they contain valuable scientific and historical information, not because we endorse every institutional decision or consider any single source complete. We encourage readers to compare information with peer-reviewed research, original historical records, independent scholarship, and community accounts, and to ask questions when perspectives or details are missing.

Our approach: Respect the evidence. Acknowledge the gaps. Center informed choice, accountability, and transparency.
