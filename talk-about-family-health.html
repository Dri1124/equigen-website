FAMILY HEALTH HISTORY · CONVERSATION GUIDE

Let's Talk About Our Family's Health

Because knowing our family's story can help protect its future.

Talking about health can feel personal. You don't need perfect words or every answer. Start with care, curiosity, and respect.

Why these conversations can be hard

Some families speak openly about health. Others value privacy, prefer not to worry loved ones, or have had difficult experiences with healthcare. Age, culture, family relationships, and personal history can all influence what feels safe to share. No single experience represents every Black family.

Your goal is understanding, not persuading someone to disclose information. A relative can say no, and you can still take steps to protect your health.

Start with four simple steps

1. Ask

Choose a calm moment and ask permission.

2. Listen

Invite stories without correcting or interrupting.

3. Respect

Let relatives decide what they want to share.

4. Revisit

Leave room for another conversation later.

What could I say?

Use these as starting points, not scripts you must follow. Adapt them to your relationship and how your family speaks.

“We don't discuss private health matters.”

“I've been learning about our family's health so I can make informed decisions. Would you feel comfortable sharing anything about conditions that run in our family?”

Ask permission, and explain why you're asking without pressuring anyone.

“I don't want you worrying about me.”

“I know you want to protect us. Knowing our family history could help us ask better questions at the doctor's office.”

Acknowledge the care behind their response.

“That's grown folks' business.”

“I respect your privacy. I'm not asking for anything you don't want to share. I'm hoping to understand any health patterns that might matter for our family.”

Honor boundaries across generations.

“I don't remember the diagnosis.”

“Do you remember how old they were when they got sick, or what happened? It's okay if you don't know the medical name.”

Family stories may offer clues even when exact diagnoses are unknown.

“We don't need to talk about that.”

“That's okay. I respect your choice. If you ever want to talk about it, I'm here.”

Don't turn a refusal into an argument.

“I want to ask, but we aren't close.”

“I'm putting together some family health information for my doctor. If you're comfortable, is there anything you think I should know?”

A short, low-pressure message may be easier than a long conversation.

What details are useful to ask about?

You can start with broad questions: “Does heart disease, high cholesterol, or cancer run in our family?” If someone is comfortable sharing, follow up with:

  • What condition was diagnosed? For cancer, what type?
  • About how old were they? An approximate age is helpful.
  • Which side of the family? Mother's, father's, or both?
  • Were there any known inherited conditions or genetic test results? Ask for details only with permission.

For EquiGen's focus areas, you might ask about very high LDL cholesterol, early heart attacks or strokes, familial hypercholesterolemia, breast or ovarian cancer, and known hereditary cancer findings.

What if I can't get answers?

It's okay to write “unknown.” Adoption, estrangement, death, incomplete records, and relatives' privacy can make family history unavailable. You do not need a complete family history to discuss screening or symptoms with a doctor. Avoid guessing at diagnoses or sharing someone's information without their permission.

Take what you learn to your healthcare appointment

Record what you know in EquiGen's Family Health History Worksheet. It already includes questions about screening, cholesterol, genetic counseling, and interpreting negative genetic tests. Bring your notes and ask your doctor what they mean for your care.

Research and trusted resources

These communication suggestions draw on family-health-history guidance and principles of respectful, person-centered conversation. Research on family communication shows that privacy, trust, and different communication preferences can shape whether relatives share health information. There is no single approach proven to work for every family.
